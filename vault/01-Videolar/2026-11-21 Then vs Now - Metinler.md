```
Then vs Now: one ordinary day in the United States around 1900. Milk and ice came by horse-drawn wagon, few homes had electricity, the toilet was usually an outhouse, the average working week was close to 60 hours, and a baby born in 1900 could expect to live about 47 years (today: nearly 80). All numbers refer to the USA, 1900.

Sources (facts):
- Life expectancy at birth, USA 1900 = 47.3 years: CDC / National Center for Health Statistics, Health, United States (life expectancy tables); about 77–79 years today (CDC/NCHS).

- Work week: average about 58.5 hours for non-farm workers in 1900 (Historical Statistics of the United States, series Ba4575); about 60 hours in manufacturing (Robert Whaples, "Hours of Work in U.S. History", EH.Net Encyclopedia).
- Indoor bathrooms: even in the 1940 US Census only about 55% of homes had complete plumbing (US Census Bureau).
```

## 2) knockerup — `video/out/gizem-knockerup-en.mp4`
**Başlık:** Before Alarm Clocks, People Paid a Knocker-Up #shorts

**Açıklama:**
```
Then vs Now: before cheap alarm clocks, workers in the industrial towns of Britain and Ireland paid a "knocker-up" to wake them. They tapped on bedroom windows with long poles before dawn; in London's East End, Mary Smith famously used a pea shooter, for sixpence a week. The Netherlands had them too ("porders"). Who woke the knocker-up? Usually nobody: many worked through the night and slept by day.

Sources (facts): "Knocker-up", Wikipedia (and sources cited there); Mary Smith of Limehouse, East London, and her pea shooter — widely reported anecdote with surviving 1930s photographs; Dutch "porder": Nationaal Archief / IISG photo captions (1947).
```

## 3) lector — `video/out/gizem-lector-en.mp4`
**Başlık:** Cigar Workers Paid Someone to Read Them Novels #shorts

**Açıklama:**
```
Then vs Now: in Cuban cigar factories, a "lector" read aloud from a platform while workers rolled cigars: newspapers in the morning, novels in the afternoon. The tradition began in Havana in 1865 and spread to Tampa, Florida (Ybor City). The workers paid the reader themselves and voted on what he read. After the 1931 strike, Tampa factory owners banned the readers. The Montecristo cigar is often said to be named after a favourite read, The Count of Monte Cristo, and Romeo y Julieta takes its name from Shakespeare. Cuba still has lectors today.

Sources (facts): Araceli Tinajero, "El Lector: A History of the Cigar Factory Reader" (University of Texas Press, 2010); Lewis Hine, 1909 LoC caption ("He is paid by them"); Ybor City Museum State Park; "Montecristo (cigar)" and "Romeo y Julieta (cigar)", Wikipedia — the Montecristo naming story is traditional, not documented.
```

## 4) humancomputers — `video/out/gizem-humancomputers-en.mp4`
**Başlık:** "Computer" Was Once a Job Title, Not a Machine #shorts

**Açıklama:**
```
Then vs Now: a "computer" used to be a person. From the 1880s, Harvard College Observatory hired women to study stars on glass photographic plates; Annie Jump Cannon alone classified about 350,000 stars. Later NACA, which became NASA, hired women computers to work through flight data by hand. Katherine Johnson calculated trajectories for early US space flights, and in 1962 John Glenn asked for her to check the electronic computer's numbers before his Friendship 7 orbital flight — reportedly saying "If she says they're good, then I'm ready to go." Glenn orbited Earth three times and came home safely.

Sources (facts and quote): NASA, "Katherine Johnson Biography" (quote as reported by NASA; wording varies between sources); Harvard College Observatory / "Harvard Computers", Wikipedia; Annie Jump Cannon — Smithsonian / Harvard Library (about 350,000 classified stars); NASA, Mercury-Atlas 6 (Friendship 7) mission, 20 Feb 1962.
```
