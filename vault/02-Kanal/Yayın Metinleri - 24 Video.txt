BAŞLIK:
Why Does Every Snowflake Have 6 Sides? ❄️ #shorts

AÇIKLAMA:
Every snowflake has six sides, and the reason is too small for your eyes to see. A water molecule looks like a tiny boomerang, and as water freezes these molecules lock together into hexagonal rings. The snowflake grows on that hexagon, and its six arms look alike because they grow in the same air at the same time. Its shape depends on temperature: a flat plate near -2 °C, a needle near -5 °C, and a branching star near -15 °C. Simple snowflakes can match, but finding a twin for a branching star is almost impossible. And snow is not really white: ice is clear, and the white comes from light scattering off thousands of tiny surfaces.

Sources: Kenneth G. Libbrecht, Caltech (snowflake and ice crystal research); National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC).

AÇIKLAMA:
Imagine rolling cigars all day, while someone reads you novels. In Cuban cigar factories this was a real job, called the lector, starting in Havana in 1865. Back then, one voice for the whole room. Today, a podcast in every ear.

"Computer" Was Once a Job Title, Not a Machine #shorts

AÇIKLAMA:
Before computers were machines, computers were people, often women with pencils. From the 1880s, Harvard Observatory hired women to study stars on glass plates, and Annie Jump Cannon alone classified about 350,000 stars. NACA, later NASA, hired women computers too, and Katherine Johnson calculated flight paths for America's first space missions. In 1962, John Glenn reportedly wanted her to check the machine's numbers: if she says they are good, he is ready to go. She did, and Glenn orbited Earth three times and came home safely. Back then, rooms full of pencils. Today, billions of sums a second, in your pocket.

Sources: NASA, Katherine Johnson biography (NASA History Office); Harvard College Observatory and the Smithsonian on the Harvard Computers; NASA Langley Research Center on the "West Computers."
